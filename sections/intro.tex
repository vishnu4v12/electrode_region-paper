\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Arc discharges find extensive applications in welding, cutting, and thermal spraying. In recent years, their use has expanded to emerging applications, including plasma pyrolysis, advanced material synthesis, and metallurgical reduction processes \cite{murphy2023handbook,aghayee2024plasma,zhang2017synthesis}. An atmospheric-pressure arc discharge spans a wide range of length scales, differing by several orders of magnitude. The millimeter-to-centimeter-scale quasi-neutral arc column constitutes the bulk of the discharge, where convection, thermal conduction, Joule heating, and radiation interact under conditions that are often close to local thermodynamic equilibrium (LTE). In contrast, the sub-micron to sub-millimeter regions adjacent to the electrodes are characterized by strong nonequilibrium effects. In these regions, the plasma is subjected to the relatively cold electrode surfaces, steep density gradients associated with particle absorption at the electrodes, and strong electric fields arising from charge separation. These fields can accelerate electrons and ions to energies substantially higher than their local thermal energies.

Resolving the full range of length scales throughout the entire discharge is neither computationally practical nor necessary for many applications. Consequently, arc-discharge models commonly treat the quasi-neutral bulk plasma separately from the nonequilibrium electrode regions. In the bulk, the quasineutrality condition, $n_i\approx n_e$, eliminates the need to explicitly solve Poisson's equation, while the near-LTE assumption further reduces model complexity by allowing thermodynamic and transport properties to be determined primarily from the local temperature and pressure. Macroscopic effects such as cathode jets, electrode geometry, convection, and electromagnetic forces can then be incorporated within a continuum formulation based on the Navier--Stokes equations with Joule-heating and Lorentz-force terms. The non-equilibrium electrode regions are generally modeled to account for diffusion, reaction, sheath effects in the discharge.

Quasi-neutral bulk plasma modeling, can be broadly classified into four categories according to the degree of thermodynamic and chemical equilibrium assumed and the treatment of electron and heavy-particle temperatures.

\emph{***(i) Full local thermodynamic equilibrium.***} Experimental measurements of species temperatures and electron densities indicate that the arc core is generally close to LTE \cite{pokrzywka1996spectroscopic,murphy1992laser,tanaka1999plasma}. In a full LTE model, electrons and heavy particles are characterized by a single temperature $T$, with Maxwellian velocity distributions, Boltzmann excitation, Saha ionization, and Dalton's law used to determine the plasma state and properties. The governing equations typically consist of mass, momentum, energy, and current continuity \cite{lago2004numerical,freton2000comparison,Hsu1983}. Conventional LTE models does not account for electrode falls and sheaths effects.

\emph{***(ii) Two-temperature models.***} Two-temperature (2T) models treat electrons and heavy particles as separate Maxwellian populations at $T_e$ and $T_h$, coupled through elastic energy exchange. In two-temperature-equilibrium (2T-EQ) models, the chemical composition is additionally determined from a generalized Saha relation for $T_e\neq T_h$. Hsu and Pfender \cite{HsuPfender1983b} showed that $T_e\approx T_h$ in the high-density arc core, with significant temperature separation emerging in the low-density fringes and near the anode. However, the generalized Saha closure is not unique: different formulations can predict electron densities differing by up to an order of magnitude away from $T_e/T_h\approx1$ \cite{Rat2008}. Furthermore, inconsistencies in the partitioning of ionization-energy and reactive thermal-conductivity terms between the electron and heavy-particle energy equations have been identified by Freeton et.al \cite{Freton2012} in several formulations. 

\emph{***(iii) Nonequilibrium models.***} Fully nonequilibrium models abandon equilibrium closures such as LTE and generalized Saha equilibrium, allowing the plasma composition and electron and heavy-particle temperatures to evolve independently. These models typically include multicomponent species transport and separate electron and heavy-particle energy equations. Examples include the nonequilibrium arc models of Haidar \cite{haidar1999non}, Gonzalez \textit{et al.} \cite{gonzalez2000decay}, and Amakawa \textit{et al.} \cite{amakawa1998anode}. By resolving departures from equilibrium directly, these models can describe the transition from the LTE-like bulk plasma toward the strongly nonequilibrium electrode region. However, the space-charge sheath is generally still treated separately using an appropriate sheath model.

\emph{***(iv) LTE with diffusion-driven ionization nonequilibrium.***} Another approach considered in the literature retains $T_e=T_h=T$ while allowing the electron and ion densities to depart from their local Saha-equilibrium values \cite{Lowke2006,Tanaka2007,murphy2009computational}. This departure arises when diffusion competes with the ionization--recombination relaxation processes, particularly near the electrodes. Lowke \cite{Lowke2006} demonstrated this approach using a full-diffusion calculation that solves the ambipolar electron-continuity equation together with current continuity, including the electron-diffusion contribution. The resulting solution produces a retarding-field potential structure near the electrode that is absent in a conventional LTE calculation. However, because $T_e=T_h$ is retained throughout the domain, this formulation cannot capture the strong electron--heavy-particle temperature separation expected in the low-density electrode-adjacent region.

\subsection{Coupling the bulk plasma to the electrode: sheath models}
\label{sec:intro-electrode}

Regardless of the bulk-plasma model employed, the bulk solution must ultimately be coupled to a description of the electrode region. The appropriate coupling strategy depends on the assumptions underlying the bulk formulation and, in particular, on the degree of nonequilibrium that is retained. For fully LTE models (i), the electrode region must account for the breakdown of LTE, including the separation between electron and heavy-particle temperatures, deviations from Saha equilibrium, and the formation of a space-charge sheath. For two-temperature and two-temperature-equilibrium models (ii), the bulk formulation already accounts for electron--heavy-particle temperature nonequilibrium; however, deviations from ionization equilibrium and the formation of a space-charge sheath must still be described. In fully nonequilibrium bulk models (iii), an additional ionization layer or presheath is not necessarily required, since departures from equilibrium are already permitted within the bulk formulation. The principal remaining transition to the electrode is therefore associated with the breakdown of quasineutrality and the formation of the space-charge sheath.

A common approach is to construct the electrode-region model from separate submodels for the ionization layer or presheath and the sheath. The ionization layer is treated as a collisional region and coupled to the sheath, which is treated as collisionless using an analytical model \cite{DinulescuPfender1980,hsu1983analysis,schmitz2002analysis,benilov1995model}. This approach avoids the direct solution of the stiff Poisson equation across the sheath. However, Benilov \textit{et al.} \cite{Benilov2016} pointed out that such matching is strictly valid only if the sheath is collision-dominated.

Almeida, Benilov, and Naidis \cite{Almeida2008} evaluated the number of ion collisions occurring as an ion traverses the cathode space-charge sheath,they pointed out that for atmospheric-pressure argon arc discharge, the ion-collision number is far greater than unity rather than \(\ll 1\). Which demonstrate that a physically consistent electrode model for high-pressure arc discharges should account for collisional ion transport within the sheath. A kinetic study performed by Li Sun et al. \cite{sun2022unified} for the near-cathode region supported the collision dominated view. Futher Semenov et al. \cite{semenov2016numerical} demonstarted that the simiple sheath models that assume a bohm velocity at the sheath edge are not valid for anode-region of high-pressure arc discharges.


An alternative approach is \emph{unified modeling}, in which a single set of governing equations, including Poisson's equation, is solved across the entire interelectrode region without explicitly separating the bulk plasma from the sheath. This approach provides a self-consistent description of the electrode region but is computationally expensive because the mesh must resolve the extremely small sheath length scales. Consequently, applications to arc discharges have largely been restricted to one-dimensional calculations extending from the electrode into the bulk plasma \cite{Almeida2008,almeida2009investigating,semenov2016numerical}.




Except in the immediate vicinity of the electrodes, an atmospheric-pressure arc is sufficiently close to local thermodynamic equilibrium (LTE) that the bulk plasma can be modeled using a single temperature. Consequently, nonequilibrium effects need not be considered in the bulk, which substantially reduces the complexity of the model.The approach adopted in the present work therefore retains the computationally efficient LTE treatment for the bulk of the arc while explicitly resolving the nonequilibrium physics in the near-electrode region. Rather than prescribing electrode-boundary quantities or relying on an analytical sheath model, a unified drift-diffusion--Poisson formulation is employed to simultaneously determine the charged-particle distributions, species fluxes, ion impact energies, heat transfer to the electrode, and sheath voltage drop. The same governing drift-diffusion--Poisson system is solved continuously from the quasi-neutral bulk plasma through the near-electrode transition region and into the sheath. The highly disparate spatial scales are resolved using adaptive mesh refinement (AMR), with the computational mesh refined to a fraction of the local Debye length in the sheath region.

An additional advantage is that one need not worry about field reversals. Field reversals and changes in the sign of the potential drop near the anode have been reported in atmospheric-pressure arcs and can depend on several factors, including the attachment mode, current density, and electrode ablation effects \cite{SandersPfender1984,nemchinsky2016anode,heberlein2010anode}. By solving the drift-diffusion--Poisson system self-consistently across the near-electrode region, the sheath potential structure and its polarity emerge from the coupled solution rather than being assumed a priori. This approach enables one to use the results from the computational efficient bulk plasma treatment to inform the boundary conditions for the near-electrode region, while simultaneously capturing the nonequilibrium effects that arise in the sheath and ionization layer. The model and its numerical implementation in the \textsc{PlasmeX} code are described in Section~\ref{sec:methods}.
