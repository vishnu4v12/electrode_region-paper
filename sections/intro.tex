\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Arc discharges find extensive applications in welding, cutting, and thermal spraying. In recent years, their use has expanded to emerging applications, including plasma pyrolysis, advanced material synthesis, and metallurgical reduction processes \cite{murphy2023handbook,aghayee2024plasma,zhang2017synthesis}. An atmospheric-pressure arc discharge spans a wide range of length scales, differing by several orders of magnitude. The millimeter-to-centimeter-scale quasi-neutral arc column constitutes the bulk of the discharge, where convection, thermal conduction, Joule heating, and radiation interact under conditions that are often close to local thermodynamic equilibrium (LTE). In contrast, the sub-micron to sub-millimeter regions adjacent to the electrodes are characterized by strong nonequilibrium effects. In these regions, the plasma is subjected to the relatively cold electrode surfaces, steep density gradients associated with particle absorption at the electrodes, and strong electric fields arising from charge separation. These fields can accelerate electrons and ions to energies substantially higher than their local thermal energies.

Resolving the full range of length scales throughout the entire discharge is neither computationally practical nor necessary for many applications. Consequently, arc-discharge models commonly treat the quasi-neutral bulk plasma separately from the nonequilibrium electrode regions. In the bulk, the quasineutrality condition, $n_i\approx n_e$, eliminates the need to explicitly solve Poisson's equation, while the near-LTE assumption further reduces model complexity by allowing thermodynamic and transport properties to be determined primarily from the local temperature and pressure. Macroscopic effects such as cathode jets, electrode geometry, convection, and electromagnetic forces can then be incorporated within a continuum formulation based on the Navier--Stokes equations with Joule-heating and Lorentz-force terms.

Quasi-neutral bulk plasma modeling, can be broadly classified into four categories according to the degree of thermodynamic and chemical equilibrium assumed and the treatment of electron and heavy-particle temperatures.

\emph{***(i) Full local thermodynamic equilibrium.***} Experimental measurements of species temperatures and electron densities indicate that the arc core is generally close to LTE \cite{pokrzywka1996spectroscopic,murphy1992laser,tanaka1999plasma}. In a full LTE model, electrons and heavy particles are characterized by a single temperature $T$, with Maxwellian velocity distributions, Boltzmann excitation, Saha ionization, and Dalton's law used to determine the plasma state and properties. The governing equations typically consist of mass, momentum, energy, and current continuity \cite{lago2004numerical,freton2000comparison,Hsu1983}. Conventional LTE models terminate before the electrode sheaths, where the assumptions of LTE and quasineutrality break down. Moreover, as $n_e\rightarrow0$ near an absorbing electrode, $\sigma\rightarrow0$, making the current-continuity equation, $\nabla\cdot(\sigma\nabla\phi)=0$, increasingly ill-conditioned as the mesh is refined. Lowke and Tanaka's LTE-diffusion approximation \cite{Lowke2006} avoids this difficulty by limiting the near-electrode cell size to the electron diffusion length, $\Lambda=D_e/W=(kT_e/e)/E_z$, while retaining LTE properties. This approach reproduces the arc voltage and electrode heat fluxes to within approximately 10\% of a full nonequilibrium treatment.

\emph{***(ii) Two-temperature and two-temperature-equilibrium models.***} Two-temperature (2T) models treat electrons and heavy particles as separate Maxwellian populations at $T_e$ and $T_h$, coupled through elastic energy exchange. In two-temperature-equilibrium (2T-EQ) models, the chemical composition is additionally determined from a generalized Saha relation for $T_e\neq T_h$. Hsu and Pfender \cite{HsuPfender1983b} showed that $T_e\approx T_h$ in the high-density arc core, with significant temperature separation emerging in the low-density fringes and near the anode. However, the generalized Saha closure is not unique: different formulations can predict electron densities differing by up to an order of magnitude away from $T_e/T_h\approx1$ \cite{Rat2008}. Furthermore, inconsistencies in the partitioning of ionization-energy and reactive thermal-conductivity terms between the electron and heavy-particle energy equations have been identified in several formulations \cite{Freton2012}.

\emph{***(iii) Nonequilibrium models.***} Fully nonequilibrium models abandon equilibrium closures such as LTE and generalized Saha equilibrium, allowing the plasma composition and electron and heavy-particle temperatures to evolve independently. These models typically include multicomponent species transport and separate electron and heavy-particle energy equations. Examples include the nonequilibrium arc models of Haidar \cite{haidar1999non}, Gonzalez \textit{et al.} \cite{gonzalez2000decay}, and Amakawa \textit{et al.} \cite{amakawa1998anode}. By resolving departures from equilibrium directly, these models can describe the transition from the LTE-like bulk plasma toward the strongly nonequilibrium electrode region. However, the space-charge sheath is generally still treated separately using an appropriate sheath model.

\emph{***(iv) LTE with diffusion-driven ionization nonequilibrium.***} Another approach considered in the literature retains $T_e=T_h=T$ while allowing the electron and ion densities to depart from their local Saha-equilibrium values. This departure arises when diffusion competes with the ionization--recombination relaxation processes, particularly near the electrodes. Lowke \cite{Lowke2006} demonstrated this approach using a full-diffusion calculation that solves the ambipolar electron-continuity equation together with current continuity, including the electron-diffusion contribution. The resulting solution produces a retarding-field potential structure near the electrode that is absent in a conventional LTE calculation. Tanaka and Lowke \cite{Tanaka2007} further incorporated Poisson's equation to account for space-charge effects and reported relatively small sheath voltage drops compared with the total arc voltage. However, because $T_e=T_h$ is retained throughout the domain, this formulation cannot capture the strong electron--heavy-particle temperature separation expected in the low-density electrode-adjacent region.

\subsection{Coupling the bulk plasma to the electrode: sheath models}
\label{sec:intro-electrode}

Regardless of the bulk-plasma model employed, the bulk solution must ultimately be coupled to a description of the electrode region. The appropriate coupling strategy depends on the assumptions made in the bulk model and on which nonequilibrium processes are retained.

For full LTE models (i), the electrode region must account for the breakdown of LTE, including the separation between electron and heavy-particle temperatures, deviations from Saha equilibrium, and the formation of a space-charge sheath. One possible approach is \emph{unified modeling}, in which a single set of governing equations, including Poisson's equation, is solved across the entire inter-electrode region without explicitly separating the bulk plasma from the sheath. This approach provides a self-consistent description of the electrode region but is computationally expensive because the mesh must resolve the extremely small sheath length scales. Consequently, applications to arc discharges have largely been restricted to one-dimensional calculations extending from the electrode into the bulk plasma \cite{Almeida2008,almeida2009investigating}.

For two-temperature and two-temperature-equilibrium models (ii), the bulk description already accounts for electron--heavy-particle temperature differences, but deviations from ionization equilibrium and the formation of a space-charge sheath must still be addressed. A common approach is therefore to introduce an \emph{ionization layer}: a thin, quasi-neutral region adjacent to the electrode that connects the bulk plasma to the sheath. This layer provides an intermediate region in which ionization equilibrium breaks down before the plasma enters the strongly nonequilibrium sheath. The sheath is then treated as a separate space-charge region between the ionization layer and the electrode, commonly using a collisionless sheath model.

For fully nonequilibrium bulk models (iii), no additional ionization layer is necessarily required because the bulk formulation already allows departures from equilibrium. The remaining transition to the electrode is associated primarily with the breakdown of quasineutrality and the formation of the space-charge sheath. The sheath can therefore be coupled directly to the nonequilibrium quasi-neutral plasma using an appropriate sheath model. However, the validity of simplified analytical sheath models and their matching conditions depends on the collisionality of the sheath. Benilov \textit{et al.} \cite{Benilov2016} noted that direct matching to an analytical sheath solution is strictly justified only under the corresponding assumptions regarding sheath collisionality.

The limitations of the commonly adopted collisionless-sheath approximation were examined directly by Almeida, Benilov, and Naidis \cite{Almeida2008}, who evaluated the number of ion collisions occurring as an ion traverses the cathode space-charge sheath, $
\int \left( \lambda_{ia}^{-1} + \frac{\bar{\nu}_{ia}}{|v_i|} \right) \, dx.$
For mercury and atmospheric-pressure argon arcs at practically relevant current densities, they obtained values ranging from approximately \(10^2\) to \(10^4\). Thus, ions can undergo a substantial number of collisions while crossing the sheath, demonstrating that the assumption of collision-free ion motion, which underlies many simplified electrode models, is generally not justified at high pressure. Even for the comparatively well-behaved atmospheric-pressure argon case, the ion-collision number is far greater than unity rather than \(\ll 1\). This result highlights the need for a self-consistent treatment of collisional ion transport in the near-electrode region.




Except in the immediate vicinity of the electrodes, an atmospheric-pressure arc is sufficiently close to local thermodynamic equilibrium (LTE) that the bulk arc column can be modeled without explicitly resolving the nonequilibrium regions in detail. The LTE-diffusion approximation of \cite{Lowke2006} demonstrates this explicitly, reproducing the arc voltage and electrode heat flux obtained from a full nonequilibrium treatment to within approximately 10\%, while retaining a single-temperature LTE closure throughout the computational domain. The temperature distributions obtained using this approach also show good agreement with experimental measurements.

The approach adopted in the present work therefore retains the computationally efficient LTE treatment for the bulk of the arc while explicitly resolving the nonequilibrium physics in the near-electrode region. Rather than prescribing electrode-boundary quantities or relying on an analytical sheath model, a unified drift-diffusion--Poisson formulation is employed to simultaneously determine the charged-particle distributions, species fluxes, ion impact energies, heat transfer to the electrode, and sheath voltage drop. The same governing drift-diffusion--Poisson system is solved continuously from the quasi-neutral bulk plasma through the near-electrode transition region and into the sheath. The highly disparate spatial scales are resolved using adaptive mesh refinement (AMR), with the computational mesh refined to a fraction of the local Debye length in the sheath region.

An additional advantage of this approach is that it does not require a separate sheath closure based on a prescribed sheath polarity. Field reversals and changes in the sign of the potential drop near the anode have been reported in atmospheric-pressure arcs and can depend on several factors, including the attachment mode, current density, and electrode ablation effects \cite{SandersPfender1984,nemchinsky2016anode,heberlein2010anode}. By solving the drift-diffusion--Poisson system self-consistently across the near-electrode region, the sheath potential structure and its polarity emerge from the coupled solution rather than being imposed a priori. This approach enables one to use the results from the computational efficient bulk plasma treatment to inform the boundary conditions for the near-electrode region, while simultaneously capturing the nonequilibrium effects that arise in the sheath and ionization layer. The model and its numerical implementation in the \textsc{PlasmeX} code are described in Section~\ref{sec:methods}.
