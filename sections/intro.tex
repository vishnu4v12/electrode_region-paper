\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Arc discharges are widely used in welding, cutting and thermal spraying, and in recent years they have found new applications in plasma pyrolysis, advanced material synthesis and metallurgical reduction \cite{boulos2023handbook,aghayee2024plasma,zhang2017synthesis,mohanta2026electrical}. An atmospheric-pressure arc spans length scales that differ by several orders of magnitude. Most of the discharge is the hot, quasi-neutral arc column, millimeters to centimeters in size, in which convection, thermal conduction, Joule heating and radiation interact under conditions often close to local thermodynamic equilibrium (LTE). Within a fraction of a millimeter of the electrodes, by contrast, the plasma is far from equilibrium. There it is in contact with relatively cold, absorbing electrode surfaces, which produce steep gradients in the species densities and temperatures. In sub-micrometer layers next to the surfaces, charge separation develops, quasi-neutrality breaks down and strong electric fields arise.

Resolving this full range of length scales over the entire discharge is neither computationally practical nor necessary for most applications. Poisson's equation, which governs the charge separation and the electric field near the electrodes, need not be solved in the bulk plasma, where quasi-neutrality holds. Conversely, the near-electrode layers are thin compared with the flow and geometric scales of the arc, so convection, electrode jets and the electrode geometry can be neglected there; these layers are dominated by diffusion, reactions and sheath effects. Arc models therefore typically combine a quasi-neutral model of the bulk plasma with a separate near-electrode model. Several strategies have been adopted for the bulk plasma, and the choice determines what the near-electrode model must describe.

\emph{(i) Local thermodynamic equilibrium.} Measurements of the species temperatures and the electron density indicate that the arc core is generally close to LTE \cite{pokrzywka1996spectroscopic,murphy1992laser,tanaka1999plasma}. In an LTE model, electrons and heavy particles share a single temperature \(T\) and have Maxwellian velocity distributions; Boltzmann excitation, Saha ionization and Dalton's law then determine the plasma state and its properties. The governing equations typically consist of mass, momentum and energy conservation together with current continuity \cite{lago2004numerical,freton2000comparison,Hsu1983}. These models are computationally efficient because the species densities and transport properties need not be solved for: they follow from the local temperature and pressure. An LTE bulk model, however, requires a near-electrode model that captures the separation of the electron and heavy-particle temperatures, the departure from Saha equilibrium, the breakdown of quasi-neutrality and the formation of the space-charge sheath \cite{Almeida2008,almeida2009investigating,semenov2016numerical}.

\emph{(ii) Two-temperature models.} Two-temperature (2T) models treat electrons and heavy particles as separate Maxwellian populations at temperatures \(T_e\) and \(T_h\), coupled through elastic energy exchange. In two-temperature equilibrium (2T-EQ) models, the composition is in addition obtained from a generalized Saha relation for \(T_e\neq T_h\). Hsu and Pfender \cite{HsuPfender1983b} showed that \(T_e\approx T_h\) in the high-density arc core, with significant temperature separation in the low-density fringes and near the anode. The generalized Saha closure is not unique, however: different formulations can predict electron densities that differ by up to an order of magnitude once \(T_e/T_h\) departs from unity \cite{Rat2008}. Freton \textit{et al.} \cite{Freton2012} have further shown that several published formulations distribute the ionization term and the reactive thermal conductivity inconsistently between the electron and heavy-particle energy equations. A 2T bulk model still requires a near-electrode model that captures the ionization layer, or presheath, where Saha equilibrium is violated, as well as the breakdown of quasi-neutrality and the formation of the space-charge sheath \cite{benilov1995model,schmitz2002analysis}.

\emph{(iii) Nonequilibrium models.} Fully nonequilibrium models abandon equilibrium closures such as LTE and generalized Saha equilibrium, and let the plasma composition and the electron and heavy-particle temperatures evolve independently. They typically include multicomponent species transport and separate electron and heavy-particle energy equations. Examples are the nonequilibrium arc models of Haidar \cite{haidar1999non}, Gonzalez \textit{et al.} \cite{gonzalez2000decay} and Amakawa \textit{et al.} \cite{amakawa1998anode}. Because they resolve departures from equilibrium directly, these models can describe the transition from the LTE-like bulk plasma to the strongly nonequilibrium electrode region. The space-charge sheath, however, is still treated separately, by a sheath model matched to the quasi-neutral plasma \cite{baeva2016novel}.

A widely used near-electrode approach treats the ionization layer, or presheath, as a collisional region and couples it to a sheath that is assumed to be collisionless and is described analytically \cite{DinulescuPfender1980,hsu1983analysis,schmitz2002analysis,benilov1995model}. This avoids solving the stiff Poisson equation across the sheath. Almeida, Benilov and Naidis \cite{Almeida2008} estimated the number of collisions that an ion undergoes while crossing the cathode space-charge sheath of an atmospheric-pressure argon arc, and found that this number is comparable to unity rather than small. Strictly speaking, the collisionless-sheath assumption is therefore unjustified. A kinetic (particle-in-cell/Monte Carlo collision) study of the near-cathode region by Sun \textit{et al.} \cite{sun2022unified} likewise supports the view that this region, sheath included, is collision-dominated. At the cathode the consequences are limited: the integral characteristics of the plasma--cathode interaction predicted by unified and sheath-matched models are generally in good agreement \cite{Almeida2008,Benilov2016}. At the anode, however, Semenov \textit{et al.} \cite{semenov2016numerical} showed that the common collisionless sheath model, in which the ion velocity at the sheath edge is set to the Bohm velocity, fails. A physically consistent electrode model for high-pressure arcs should therefore account for collisional ion transport within the sheath.

The alternative is \emph{unified modeling}, in which a single set of governing equations, including Poisson's equation, is solved across the whole near-electrode region \cite{Almeida2008,semenov2016numerical,sun2022unified,sitaraman2026elucidating}. Ion collisions in the sheath are then accounted for by construction. Because a unified model captures both thermal and ionization nonequilibrium, it can be matched directly to an LTE model of the bulk plasma. Nor do field reversals need to be anticipated. Field reversals and changes in the sign of the potential drop near the anode have been reported in atmospheric-pressure arcs, and they depend on several factors, including the attachment mode, the current density and electrode ablation \cite{SandersPfender1984,nemchinsky2016anode,heberlein2010anode,baeva2016novel}. When the drift-diffusion--Poisson system is solved self-consistently across the near-electrode region, the potential structure of the sheath and its polarity emerge from the solution instead of being assumed a priori. Unified models are computationally expensive, however, because the mesh must resolve the very small length scales of the sheath. Consequently, their application to arc discharges has largely been restricted to one-dimensional calculations extending from the electrode into the bulk plasma \cite{Almeida2008,almeida2009investigating,krivtsun2025model,semenov2016numerical}.

In this paper we adopt the decomposition described above, treating the quasi-neutral bulk plasma and the near-electrode region with separate models, and present a transient near-electrode model intended to supply the electrode boundary conditions for our LTE model of the bulk arc \cite{korsipati2026fast}. The near-electrode model is a unified drift-diffusion--Poisson formulation that determines simultaneously the charged-particle distributions, the species fluxes, the sheath voltage drop and the heat flux to the electrode. The same system of equations is solved continuously from the quasi-neutral bulk plasma at one end of the domain, through the near-electrode transition region, and into the sheath. In previous unified models \cite{Almeida2008,krivtsun2025model,almeida2009investigating,semenov2016numerical}, the bulk-plasma state at the outer boundary was taken from the asymptotic arc column, in which Joule heating is balanced by radiation alone, without radial losses. The computational domain therefore had to extend far into the arc, where the temperature and density gradients could be assumed to vanish. In the present model, the bulk state is instead supplied at the outer boundary, from measurements or from an LTE model of the arc, and radial losses are included so that this state is consistent with the energy balance of the column. Gradients of the plasma properties may exist at this boundary; the only requirement is that the plasma there be reasonably close to LTE. The domain therefore need not extend far into the arc, which is especially helpful in multidimensional simulations. The disparate length scales involved (Section~\ref{sec:scales involved}) are resolved with adaptive mesh refinement (AMR), with the mesh in the sheath refined to a fraction of the local Debye length. The model and its numerical implementation are described in Section~\ref{sec:model}. The model is validated in Section~\ref{sec:validation} and applied to the near-cathode and near-anode layers of a high-intensity argon arc in Section~\ref{sec:results}.
