\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

The model is now applied to the cathode and anode regions of a high-intensity argon arc at atmospheric pressure. Experiments and LTE models of such arcs give the state of the plasma near the electrodes. On the basis of the scale estimates of Section~\ref{sec:scales involved} and of previous studies \cite{Almeida2008,DinulescuPfender1980,tashiro2010numerical,semenov2016numerical}, the plasma is assumed to be in LTE at a distance of \(5\times10^{-4}\)~m from the electrodes. At this distance, \(x=L\), we take \(T_{\rm bulk}=21\,000\)~K and \(J_{\rm app}=10^{8}\ \mathrm{A\,m^{-2}}\) for the cathode, and \(T_{\rm bulk}=15\,000\)~K and \(J_{\rm app}=4\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{A\,m^{-2}}\) for the anode which are typical values on the axis of a 200 amps arc \cite{Hsu1983,haddad1984temperature,lago2004numerical,LiangTrelles,korsipati2026fast}. All densities and temperatures at \(x=L\) are supplied as Dirichlet values for both cases (Section~\ref{sec:model-bc}). Unlike in the validation cases of Section~\ref{sec:validation}, the radial losses ~(\ref{eq:radial-loss}) are included, with \(T_{\rm edge}=1000\)~K. \rev{The column radius is that of a column carrying the arc current \(I=200\)~A at the applied current density, \(R=\sqrt{I/(\pi J_{\rm app})}\) (Section~\ref{sec:model-governing}), which gives \(R=0.80\)~mm at the cathode and \(R=3.99\)~mm at the anode. The small cathode radius reflects the attachment of the arc in a hot spot \cite{Hsu1983,LiangTrelles}, and the larger anode radius its wider attachment \cite{Hsu1983,lago2004numerical,korsipati2026fast}.} Bulk-plasma at the cathode is almost fully ionized, and the reactive thermal conductivity \(\kappa_R=0.03\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\) is negligible compared with electron thermal conductivity \(\kappa_e=2.6\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\). At anode however, \(\kappa_R=0.88\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\), is comparable to \(\kappa_e=1.27\) and exceeds \(\kappa_h=0.12\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\).

The LTE models and experiments from which the boundary conditions are obtained uses copper as anode and thoriated tungsten as cathode, and the same has been used in this work. The anode has a work function of \(4.65~\mathrm{eV}\) and a prescribed, uniform surface temperature \(T_{\rm wall}=1000~\mathrm{K}\). The cathode surface temperature follows from the heat balance~(\ref{eq:bc-heat-balance}), with the uniform properties \(k_w=100~\mathrm{W\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\), \(L_w=1~\mathrm{cm}\) and \(T_0=1000~\mathrm{K}\), and the emission parameters \(A_R=3\times10^4~\mathrm{A\,m^{-2}\,K^{-2}}\) and \(W=2.63~\mathrm{eV}\). The computational mesh as described in Section~\ref{sec:numerics} is employed. Table \ref{tab:base} summarizes the supplied and computed parameters for the cathode and anode cases.

\subsection{Structure of the near-electrode layers}
\label{sec:res-structure}


\emph{Cathode.} A space-charge sheath \rev{\(0.56\ \mu\)m} thick forms at the surface. In it, \(n_i\) exceeds \(n_e\), by up to a factor of \rev{2.6} at the surface (figure~\ref{fig:base-profiles}(a)), and across it the potential rises by \rev{3.1~V}. The field at the surface is \rev{\(1.6\times10^7\ \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}\)}, and it falls by an order of magnitude across the sheath (figure~\ref{fig:base-profiles}(e)). Beyond the sheath, the potential keeps rising through the quasi-neutral ionization layer, to \rev{7.9~V} at \(x=10^{-4}\)~m. Further out, the plasma approaches the bulk state, and the remaining rise to \rev{11.7~V} at \(x=L\) is mostly the ohmic drop of the column, whose field is \rev{\(7.5\times10^3\ \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}\)}. The electron temperature \rev{varies little across the whole layer, 17.1--22.6~kK. It is highest, 22.6~kK, at \(x\approx0.36\)~mm, above \(T_{\rm bulk}\), and falls to \(T_{\rm bulk}\) at \(x=L\).} The heavy-particle temperature rises from the surface value of 3.7~kK and approaches \(T_e\) only at \(x\approx10^{-4}\)~m (figure~\ref{fig:base-profiles}(c)). Over the same distance, the plasma becomes almost fully ionized. Close to the cathode, the charged-particle density is far above the equilibrium density \(n_{\rm eq}(T_h)\) that the reactions would give at the local heavy-particle temperature (figure~\ref{fig:base-profiles}(a)), by a factor of \rev{360} at \(10\ \mu\)m. The two densities come within a factor of 2 of each other beyond \rev{\(32\ \mu\)m}, and within 1\,\% only beyond \rev{\(0.19\)~mm}, where \(T_h\) has approached \(T_e\). The emitted electron flux, \rev{\(1.11\times10^{27}\ \mathrm{m^{-2}\,s^{-1}}\)}, corresponds to a current density of \rev{\(1.79\times10^8\ \mathrm{A\,m^{-2}}\), 1.79 times} the arc current density. It is partly offset by the plasma electrons that reach the surface against the sheath field, \rev{\(5.2\times10^{26}\ \mathrm{m^{-2}\,s^{-1}}\)} (table~\ref{tab:base}), and the ions carry \rev{4.8\,\%} of the current at the surface (figure~\ref{fig:base-profiles}(e)).

\emph{Anode.} The space-charge sheath is thin. The charge separation exceeds 1\,\% only within \(1.0\ \mu\)m of the surface, across which the potential changes by \rev{1.39~V}. In this sheath the ions are in excess, and at the surface \(n_i\) exceeds \(n_e\) by a factor of 3.1 (figure~\ref{fig:base-profiles}(b)). The anode lies \rev{5.22~V} below the bulk-plasma boundary, and most of this drop is distributed over the quasi-neutral layer, with \rev{2.38~V} within \(10\ \mu\)m and \rev{3.44~V} within \(100\ \mu\)m. The field is directed towards the anode, \rev{\(3.8\times10^6\ \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}\)} at the surface (figure~\ref{fig:base-profiles}(f)), and retards the electrons, which reach the surface by diffusion down the steep density gradient. Across the layer, the charged-particle density falls by a factor of \rev{870}, from \(1.8\times10^{23}\) to \(2.1\times10^{20}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}\). The charged-particle densities exceed the equilibrium density \(n_{\rm eq}(T_h)\) by a factor of \rev{33} at \(100\ \mu\)m, \rev{2.3} at \(200\ \mu\)m and 1.2 at \(300\ \mu\)m (figure~\ref{fig:base-profiles}(b)), while closer to the anode \(n_{\rm eq}(T_h)\) is negligible. The anode layer is thus over-populated with charged particles, which recombine there, and \(n_e\) comes within 10\,\% of \(n_{\rm eq}(T_h)\) only within \(0.15\)~mm of \(x=L\). The electron and heavy-particle temperatures decrease together towards the anode, but \(T_h\) falls faster, to the prescribed surface temperature of 1~kK, so the two separate progressively, by 1.3~kK at \(x=10^{-4}\)~m, 4.2~kK at \(x=10^{-5}\)~m and 5.1~kK at the surface, where \(T_e=6.1\)~kK (figure~\ref{fig:base-profiles}(d)). The ions flow towards the anode across the whole layer, \(j_i>0\). They carry 2.7\,\% of the current at the surface, and their share grows towards the bulk plasma, to 13\,\% near \(x=L\) (figure~\ref{fig:base-profiles}(f)).

\subsection{Electron energy balance}
\label{sec:res-energy}

Figure~\ref{fig:base-budget} shows every term of the steady electron energy equation~(\ref{eq:eenergy}). The divergence of the electron energy flux~(\ref{eq:heatflux-e}), \(-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{h}_e\), is plotted as one term that combines the enthalpy flux, conduction and the thermal-diffusion heat flux. The volume terms are Joule heating \(-e\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_e\cdot\mathbf{E}\), elastic exchange with the heavy particles, the ionization term \(-\varepsilon_{\rm ion}S\), radiation \(-w_{\rm rad}\) and the radial loss \(-Q_e\)~(\ref{eq:radial-loss}). Positive terms heat the electrons, and at steady state the terms sum to zero. The balance is very different at the two electrodes.

\emph{Cathode.} Joule heating is the only source of electron energy, and it is the largest term everywhere (figure~\ref{fig:base-budget}(a)). It peaks at the surface, \rev{\(1.5\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-3}}\)}, where the sheath field accelerates the emitted electrons, and falls by more than two orders of magnitude within \(10\ \mu\)m. Over this distance it is balanced almost entirely by \(-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{h}_e\), which takes up \rev{97\,\%} of the Joule heat released there. In the sheath alone, the two cancel to within 0.2\,\%, and the volume losses there are two to four orders of magnitude smaller than the Joule heating. The Joule heat released near the surface is therefore not lost locally but is carried by the electron energy flux to the cathode and towards the bulk. Beyond \(10\ \mu\)m, \(-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{h}_e\) is small, and the Joule heating, now \(10^{12}\)--\(10^{13}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-3}}\), is balanced by the volume terms. Ionization is the largest of them out to about \rev{\(90\ \mu\)m}, with a peak of \rev{\(1.4\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-3}}\) at \(7\ \mu\)m}. The elastic exchange peaks at \rev{\(6.2\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-3}}\)} at \(20\ \mu\)m, where it heats the heavy particles (figure~\ref{fig:base-profiles}(c)). Both terms become small beyond \(200\ \mu\)m, where \(T_h\) has approached \(T_e\) and the plasma is close to ionization equilibrium. In the column part of the domain, \(x>100\ \mu\)m, the radial loss, \rev{the electron energy flux towards \(x=L\)} and, to a lesser extent, radiation take up the Joule heating. Of the Joule heat released over the whole domain, \rev{58\,\%} leaves as electron energy flux into the column at \(x=L\), and \rev{12\,\%} into the cathode with the absorbed plasma electrons. The radial loss takes \rev{15\,\%}, most of it at \(x>100\ \mu\)m, and ionization 7\,\%. \rev{Radiation takes 5\,\% and elastic exchange 3\,\%.}

\emph{Anode.} The Joule term is a loss throughout the domain (figure~\ref{fig:base-budget}(b)), because the electrons flow towards the anode against the retarding field. Its magnitude is largest at the surface, \(1.5\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-3}}\). Apart from recombination, the only source of electron energy is \(-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{h}_e\), which is positive everywhere. Only 14\,\% of the electron energy flux that enters at \(x=L\) reaches the surface. Within \(10\ \mu\)m of the anode, \(-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{h}_e\) and the Joule loss balance each other to within 1\,\%, and this region accounts for \rev{45\,\%} of the Joule loss over the domain. At the surface, the other volume terms are five orders of magnitude smaller. Further from the anode, the Joule loss decreases to about \(10^{10}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-3}}\), and the volume terms become comparable to it. The elastic loss to the heavy particles peaks at \rev{\(3.7\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-3}}\)} at \(57\ \mu\)m, where \(T_e-T_h\) is still 2.2~kK. The ionization term heats the electrons, with a peak of \(2.0\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-3}}\) at \rev{\(199\ \mu\)m}. Here the plasma recombines, \(S<0\), because the charged particles are supplied by diffusion at densities well above the local ionization equilibrium (Section~\ref{sec:val-anode}), and the recombination energy is returned to the electrons. Radiation and the radial loss grow towards the bulk and reach \(1.0\times10^{10}\) and \rev{\(7.6\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-3}}\)} at \(x=L\). Over the whole domain, \(-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{h}_e\) supplies 80\,\% of the electron energy and recombination heating 20\,\%. Of this energy, the Joule loss takes \rev{67\,\%}, the elastic loss 23\,\%, the radial loss \rev{6\,\%} and radiation 4\,\%.

\subsection{Momentum balance and collisionality of the sheaths}
\label{sec:res-momentum}
% Figures and numbers: figures/results_fig/base_sheath.py (output in base_sheath_report.txt), same plotfiles.

The fluxes~(\ref{eq:flux-e}) and (\ref{eq:flux-i}) follow from the force balances~(\ref{eq:mom-e}) and (\ref{eq:mom-i}), in which the inertia of each species relative to the heavy gas is neglected. Figure~\ref{fig:base-momentum} shows the terms of these balances across the domain in the two steady states, namely the electric force, the pressure gradient, the two collisional drags combined and the thermal force. In the steady states the heavy gas is at rest, so the share \(Y_s\mathbf{F}\) of the net force is negligible. For comparison, the figure also shows the inertia \(m_sn_s(\mathbf{u}_s\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}_s\) that the model neglects, evaluated a posteriori from the computed fluxes. The retained terms sum to zero to within \(4\times10^{-3}\) of the largest one.

\emph{Electrons.} In both sheaths the field retards the electrons, and the electric force on them is balanced by their pressure gradient (figure~\ref{fig:base-momentum}(c), (d)). In the sheath the two cancel to within \rev{0.9\,\%} (cathode) and 1.3\,\% (anode) of the electric force. The drag and the thermal force are small there. The drag becomes important only in the quasi-neutral layer, where it carries the current. At \(10\ \mu\)m from the surface it is 14\,\% (cathode) and 5.0\,\% (anode) of the electric force, and at the anode the thermal force is as large there. The inertia of the electrons exceeds their drag within \(0.33\ \mu\)m of the cathode (by up to a factor of \rev{34}) and within \(0.40\ \mu\)m of the anode (by up to a factor of 22), but it remains small compared with the electric force and the pressure gradient, which dominate the balance. Figure~\ref{fig:base-sheath}(a), (b) shows the electron and ion mean free paths \(\lambda_e\) and \(\lambda_i\) of table~\ref{tab:scales}, evaluated with the local densities and temperatures, together with the electron Debye length. The electron mean free path is of order microns, \rev{\(2.4\ \mu\)m} at the cathode sheath edge and \(2.4\ \mu\)m at the anode one. The sheaths, \rev{\(0.56\)} and \(1.0\ \mu\)m thick, therefore contain only 0.18 and 0.30 electron mean free paths, and the electrons cross both practically without collisions. Their balance in the sheaths is, however, not one of collisional transport. The drag is a few per cent of the electric force, and the field and the pressure gradient balance each other as in the Boltzmann equilibrium of a Maxwellian population retarded by a potential barrier, which does not require collisions. Simplified sheath models describe the electrons in the sheath by this Boltzmann relation, and figure~\ref{fig:base-sheath}(c), (d) tests it against the computed sheaths with \(n_B=n_e(x_{\rm s})\exp[(e/k_B)\int_{\phi_{\rm s}}^{\phi}{\rm d}\phi'/T_e]\), started at the sheath edge. The electron density follows the Boltzmann relation closely. The relation reproduces \(n_e\) to within \rev{1.8\,\%} across the cathode sheath, where \(n_e\) falls by a factor of \rev{7.3}, and to within 0.6\,\% across the anode sheath, where it falls by a factor of 13. Outside the anode sheath, it still holds to within 6\,\% up to \(10\ \mu\)m from the surface, but \(n_e\) is 16\,\% below \(n_B\) at \(30\ \mu\)m and 36\,\% below at \(100\ \mu\)m. At the cathode, it fails progressively in the ionization layer, where the electron drag carries the current, and \(n_e\) is \rev{12\,\%} below \(n_B\) at \(10\ \mu\)m and \rev{59\,\%} below at \(100\ \mu\)m. The electron description of Section \ref{sec:model-governing} therefore remains adequate in the sheaths.

\emph{Ions.} In both sheaths, the electric force on the ions is balanced by the drag (figure~\ref{fig:base-momentum}(a), (b)). At the cathode, the pressure gradient adds at most \rev{24\,\%} to the electric force in the sheath, and \rev{8\,\%} at the surface. At the anode, the field is weaker, and the pressure gradient, which also drives the ions towards the surface, is \rev{17\,\%} of the electric force at the surface and at most 22\,\% in the sheath. The thermal force on the ions is negligible, below 1\,\% of the drag in either sheath. The neglected inertia is not small inside the cathode sheath. It exceeds the drag within \rev{\(0.27\ \mu\)m} of the surface, by a factor of \rev{1.9} at the surface, stays above 10\,\% of the drag out to \(1.9\ \mu\)m, and falls below 1\,\% beyond \rev{\(11\ \mu\)m}. At the anode, the inertia is at most 69\,\% of the drag (at the surface), and below 10\,\% beyond \(0.45\ \mu\)m. The ions are more collisional than the electrons. At the sheath edge ( Figure~\ref{fig:base-sheath}(a), (b)), \(\lambda_i=\rev{0.29}\ \mu\)m at the cathode and \(0.11\ \mu\)m at the anode, so that the ions cross \rev{1.8} and 9.4 mean free paths in the sheaths. The anode sheath is thus collision-dominated for the ions, consistent with their inertia, which is below 10\,\% of the drag except within \(0.5\ \mu\)m of the surface. The cathode sheath is transitional, since the ions make one to two collisions across it and their inertia exceeds the drag over its inner half. There the ions cannot follow the steep rise of the field, and the local relation between field and ion velocity in equation~(\ref{eq:flux-i}) overestimates the ion velocity near the surface. Simplified sheath models impose the Bohm criterion on the ions entering the sheath, \(u_i\ge u_B\), with \(u_B=\sqrt{k_B(T_e+T_h)/M}\). The computed ions do not satisfy it at the sheath edge (figure~\ref{fig:base-sheath}(c), (d), right axis). At the cathode they enter the sheath at \rev{\(u_i=1540\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)}, against \rev{\(u_B=2110\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)}, i.e.\ at \rev{0.73}\,\(u_B\). They reach \(u_B\) only \rev{\(0.33\ \mu\)m} from the surface, inside the sheath, where the charge separation is already \rev{4.5\,\%}, and arrive at the surface at \rev{2.2}\,\(u_B\). At the anode, the ions enter the sheath at 0.20\,\(u_B\) and reach the surface at 0.84\,\(u_B\), never attaining \(u_B\). The Bohm flux \(n_iu_B\) at the sheath edge would therefore overestimate the ion flux entering the sheath by \rev{37\,\%} at the cathode and by a factor of 5 at the anode. The Bohm criterion is derived for a collisionless sheath. In these sheaths the ions collide with the atoms throughout the presheath and the sheath, and, except in the inner part of the cathode sheath, their velocity is set by the balance of the electric force and the drag rather than by the potential drop through which they have fallen.

\emph{A single heavy-particle temperature.} The model describes the ions and the atoms by one temperature \(T_h\). This requires, first, that the two species exchange energy over a distance \(\lambda_{T_h}=1/[(n_i+n_n)\overline{Q}_{in}]\) (figure~\ref{fig:base-sheath}(a), (b)) short compared with the length \(L_{T_h}=T_h/|\nabla T_h|\) over which \(T_h\) changes. This holds in both layers, where \(\lambda_{T_h}/L_{T_h}\) is at most \rev{0.09} at the cathode (at the surface) and 0.025 at the anode. Second, the ions must not gain energy from the field faster than they share it with the atoms. The two-temperature closure (Appendix~\ref{app:transport}) measures this heating by the effective ion temperature \(T_{\rm eff}\), which rises above \(T_h\) where the drift of the ions through the atoms is fast (figure~\ref{fig:base-sheath}(e), (f)). At the anode, the condition is satisfied except next to the surface, where \(T_{\rm eff}\) is 2.6 times \(T_h\). In the cathode sheath, the two temperatures diverge, and \(T_{\rm eff}\) exceeds \(T_h\) by a factor of 2.0 at the sheath edge and of \rev{9.9} at the surface. These values are, however, not accurate, because the closure assumes many collisions, whereas the ions cross the cathode sheath with only one to two. The single heavy-particle temperature model therefore is accurate throughout most of the near-electrode region, with minor deviations in the anode sheath and more pronounced deviations in the cathode sheath.

\subsection{Heat flux to the electrode}
\label{sec:res-heat}

The terms of the electrode heat flux~(\ref{eq:bc-qwall}) are shown in figure~\ref{fig:base-qwall}. At the cathode, the heat flux is the small difference of large terms. The absorbed plasma electrons (\rev{\(466\ \mathrm{MW\,m^{-2}}\)}), gas conduction (\rev{\(82\ \mathrm{MW\,m^{-2}}\)}) and ion recombination (\rev{\(64\ \mathrm{MW\,m^{-2}}\)}) heat the surface by \rev{\(611\ \mathrm{MW\,m^{-2}}\)} in total, and emission cools it by \rev{\(584\ \mathrm{MW\,m^{-2}}\)}, so that only \rev{\(q_w=27.2\ \mathrm{MW\,m^{-2}}\)} is conducted into the cathode body. The surface temperature, \rev{3714~K}, is therefore fixed mainly by the condition that emission cooling nearly balances the heating, and only weakly by the conduction into the cathode body. Ion recombination, at \(\varepsilon_{\rm ion}-W=13.1\)~eV per ion, provides \rev{10\,\%} of the heating, although the ions carry only \rev{4.8\,\%} of the current. Equation~(\ref{eq:bc-qwall}) includes only the recombination energy of the ions. It assumes that their kinetic energy is returned to the neutrals at the surface. In industrial processes, however, the ion impact energy can play a significant role. We estimate the ion impact energy from the directed kinetic energy \(\tfrac12Mv_d^2\), using the ion drift velocity at the surface, \(v_d=\Gamma_i/n_i=\rev{4.6}\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\). This corresponds to a drift velocity \rev{2.2} times the local Bohm velocity \(u_B\) and an ion impact energy of \rev{4.4~eV}.


At the anode, \rev{\(q_w=35.7\ \mathrm{MW\,m^{-2}}\)}, of which the absorbed electrons deliver 66\,\%, 19.1~\(\mathrm{MW\,m^{-2}}\) as the work function released on entering the metal and 4.3~\(\mathrm{MW\,m^{-2}}\) as kinetic energy. Gas conduction contributes \rev{\(11.1\ \mathrm{MW\,m^{-2}}\)}, 31\,\%, and ion recombination \rev{3.4\,\%}. The anode heat flux is thus set by the electron current through \(W\), by the electron temperature at the surface and, to a large part, by conduction in the heavy gas. Since the surface temperature is prescribed, this is the heat that the cooling of the anode removes. The ions carry 2.7\,\% of the current and fall through \(\phi_{\rm s}=1.4\)~V in a collision-dominated sheath, the ions reach the surface at \(v_d=1.0\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\), or 0.84\,\(u_B\) with an impact energy of 0.22~eV.

% \emph{Ion impact energy.} Equation~(\ref{eq:bc-qwall}) charges the ions with their recombination energy only, not with the kinetic energy with which they strike the surface. In the model, the ion energy follows from the local drift of the ions through the atoms by the two-temperature closure (Appendix~\ref{app:transport}), which presumes that the ions make many collisions over the distance in which the field changes. The cathode sheath does not satisfy this, since the ions cross it with only one to two collisions, and their inertia exceeds the drag over its inner part (Section~\ref{sec:res-momentum}). The local mean ion energy at the cathode surface is therefore not a reliable impact energy, and its distribution would require a kinetic treatment of the sheath. We therefore give only the maximum impact energy at the cathode, which is that of an ion that enters the sheath with the mean energy at the sheath edge, \(\tfrac32k_BT_{\rm eff}+\tfrac12Mv_d^2=1.6\)~eV (equations~(\ref{eq:mu-in}) and (\ref{eq:wannier})), and crosses it without a collision, gaining \(e\phi_{\rm s}=3.7\)~eV. This maximum is 5.3~eV per ion, and with the ion flux of table~\ref{tab:base} it delivers at most \(28\ \mathrm{MW\,m^{-2}}\), comparable to \(q_w\) itself. Added to the heating, however, this term would raise \(T_{\rm wall}\) by only about 20~K, since most of it would be removed by emission cooling, and \(q_w\) by less than 1\,\%. At the anode, where the ions carry 2.7\,\% of the current and fall through 1.4~V in a collision-dominated sheath, the same estimate gives at most 1.6~eV per ion and \(0.2\ \mathrm{MW\,m^{-2}}\), which is negligible.

On a thermionic cathode, the arc usually attaches in a spot with a high temperature and current density \cite{Hsu1983,LiangTrelles}. The anode attachment, which is generally more important in plasma processing, can be either constricted or diffuse, depending on the arc current, the gas flow and the anode material \cite{heberlein2010anode}. The arc temperature and the current density in front of the anode vary depending on the attachment. To study the effect of these two parameters, the anode case of table~\ref{tab:base} was repeated with the bulk temperature varied at fixed current density, \(T_{\rm bulk}=13\,000\)--\(17\,000\)~K at \(J_{\rm app}=4\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{A\,m^{-2}}\), and with the current density varied at fixed bulk temperature, \(J_{\rm app}=3\times10^{6}\)--\(10^{7}\ \mathrm{A\,m^{-2}}\) at \(T_{\rm bulk}=15\,000\)~K (figure~\ref{fig:anode-sweep}). \rev{The column radius follows the current density, \(R=\sqrt{I/(\pi J_{\rm app})}\) with \(I=200\)~A. It is 3.99~mm over the temperature series and decreases from 4.61 to 2.52~mm over the current series.} Figure~\ref{fig:anode-sweep}(a)--(f) shows which profiles are set by the bulk temperature and which by the current.

\emph{Heavy-particle temperature.} The \(T_h\) profile is set by \(T_{\rm bulk}\) over the whole domain and is \rev{nearly} independent of the current (figure~\ref{fig:anode-sweep}(a), (b)). Over the current sweep, \(T_h\) varies by less than \rev{3.0\,\%}, whereas it increases with a increase in the bulk temperature.

\emph{Electron temperature.} \(T_e\) is set by \(T_{\rm bulk}\), and is almost independent of current except within a few tens of micrometers from the anode. The current changes \(T_e\) by more than 4\,\% only within about \rev{\(60\ \mu\)m} of the anode. At the surface, \(T_e\) falls from 6.6 to \rev{5.2~kK} as \(J_{\rm app}\) increases from \(3\times10^{6}\) to \(10^{7}\ \mathrm{A\,m^{-2}}\), whereas it rises from 5.0 to \rev{7.1~kK} between 13\,000 and 17\,000~K.

\emph{Charged-particle densities.} The densities are set by \(T_{\rm bulk}\) across the layer and by the current next to the anode (figure~\ref{fig:anode-sweep}(c), (d)). At \(10\ \mu\)m from the anode, \(n_e\) rises by a factor of 3.5 between 13\,000 and 17\,000~K, from \rev{\(7.5\times10^{21}\)} to \(2.6\times10^{22}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}\), but changes by less than \rev{6\,\%} over the current sweep. Within a few micrometers of the anode, the order is reversed. The electrons that carry the current are absorbed at the thermal flux \(\tfrac14n_e\bar c_e\)~(\ref{eq:bc-electron}), so that a larger current requires a larger electron density at the surface. There \(n_e\) rises by a factor of 3.6 with the current, from \(1.5\times10^{20}\) to \(5.6\times10^{20}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}\), but decreases by 12\,\% with the bulk temperature. The ion density at the surface rises with both, by a factor of 2.9 with \(T_{\rm bulk}\), from \rev{\(3.6\times10^{20}\)} to \(1.0\times10^{21}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}\), and by \rev{24\,\%} with the current.

\emph{Potential and sheath.} The potential depends on both parameters (figure~\ref{fig:anode-sweep}(e), (f)). The voltage across the layer, \(\phi(L)\), increases with the bulk temperature, from 4.0 to 5.9~V, and decreases with the current, from 5.6 to 3.9~V. The sheath drop \(\phi_{\rm s}\) follows the same trends, rising from \rev{0.81 to 1.90~V} with \(T_{\rm bulk}\) and falling from \rev{1.70 to 0.73~V} with the current (figure~\ref{fig:anode-sweep}(l), (n)). The sheath becomes thinner with both parameters (figure~\ref{fig:anode-sweep}(k), (m)), from 1.12 to \(0.91\ \mu\)m with \(T_{\rm bulk}\) and from 1.07 to \(0.77\ \mu\)m with the current, as the ion density at the surface rises.

\emph{Heat flux.} The heat flux to the anode increases linearly with the current density, from \rev{30.8 to \(68.0\ \mathrm{MW\,m^{-2}}\)} between \(3\times10^{6}\) and \(10^{7}\ \mathrm{A\,m^{-2}}\) (figure~\ref{fig:anode-sweep}(i)). It has two parts of different origin. The electron terms are proportional to the current. The work function released by the absorbed electrons contributes 4.70--4.83~W per ampere, slightly more than \(W/e\) because the ions flow into the anode and the electron current exceeds the arc current by \(j_i\), and their kinetic energy adds \rev{0.91--1.19~W} per ampere. Gas conduction, in contrast, does not depend on the current. It stays between \rev{10.7 and \(11.4\ \mathrm{MW\,m^{-2}}\)} because the heavy-particle temperature profile is set by the bulk temperature. At fixed current density, \(q_w\) rises from 29.5 to \rev{\(42.6\ \mathrm{MW\,m^{-2}}\)} between 13\,000 and 17\,000~K (figure~\ref{fig:anode-sweep}(g)). Most of this increase comes from gas conduction, which rises from 6.8 to \rev{\(15.5\ \mathrm{MW\,m^{-2}}\)}, and from ion recombination (0.4 to \(2.4\ \mathrm{MW\,m^{-2}}\)). The electron terms rise by 11\,\%, with \(T_e\) at the surface rising from 5.0 to \rev{7.1~kK}.

\emph{Ion current.} The ions flow into the anode in every case. Their current density at the surface hardly depends on the arc current. While the current density increases by a factor of 3.3, \(j_i\) stays between \rev{\(0.95\) and \(1.19\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{A\,m^{-2}}\)} (figure~\ref{fig:anode-sweep}(j)), so that the ion share of the current falls from \rev{4.0} to 1.0\,\%. Like the heavy-particle temperature and the density of the layer, it is set instead by the bulk temperature. Between 13\,000 and 17\,000~K, \(j_i\) increases by a factor of 5.8, from \(0.37\times10^{5}\) to \rev{\(2.2\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{A\,m^{-2}}\)} (figure~\ref{fig:anode-sweep}(h)), and the ion share of the current from 0.9 to \rev{5.4\,\%}.

In summary, the bulk temperature sets the heavy-particle temperature, the charged-particle density of the layer, the ion current and the gas conduction to the anode, while the current sets the electron density and, to a lesser extent, the electron temperature next to the anode, and the electron terms of the heat flux. The voltage across the layer, the sheath and the total heat flux depend on both.

It is useful to know under which conditions the surface quantities can be considered quasi-steady, because this decides whether a transient model of the near-electrode region is needed or whether a steady-state model \cite{Almeida2008,Benilov2016,semenov2016numerical} can be coupled to an LTE model of the arc. Here we estimate how slowly the current and the bulk temperature must change for the ion current density \(j_i\) and the heat flux \(q_w\) at the surface to remain quasi-steady, that is, to follow the steady state at the instantaneous conditions to a given accuracy. 



In the one-dimensional model, the pressure disturbances launched by a change of the current or of the bulk temperature can travel only along the axis, and they are reflected at the electrode, which delays the approach to the new steady state. In a real arc, however, these disturbances are relieved radially. We therefore remove the acoustic response in every case by multiplying the inertia of the heavy gas in equation~(\ref{eq:heavy-momentum}) by \(\epsilon=10^{-4}\), which increases the speed of sound by a factor of \(1/\sqrt{\epsilon}=100\). This changes the steady-state quantities by less than 0.3\,\%.

The response of a surface quantity \(f\), either \(j_i\) or \(q_w\), to a step \(\Delta X\) of \(X=J_{\rm app}\) or \(T_{\rm bulk}\) is measured in units of the step,
\begin{equation}
g(t)=\frac{f(t)/f_0-1}{\Delta X/X},
\label{eq:g}
\end{equation}
where \(\Delta X/X=0.02\) is the relative size of the step and \(f_0\) is the steady value before the step. The final value of \(g\), denoted by \(g_\infty\), is the sensitivity of the steady state to \(X\). The duration of the response, \(t_{\rm s}\), is the time after which \(|g-g_\infty|\) stays below 0.01. Both are listed in table~\ref{tab:qs} for the two cases of table~\ref{tab:base}. We assume that the response is linear, so that \(g\) depends neither on the size nor on the sign of the step. For the cases of table~\ref{tab:base}, steps of \(+2\) and \(-2\,\%\) give values of the coefficient \(D_X\) defined below within 5\,\% of their mean.

Suppose now that \(X\) changes slowly, starting at \(t=0\) from a steady state in which \(f=f_0\). Since the response is linear, it is the superposition of the responses to the small steps that make up the change,
\begin{equation}
\frac{f(t)-f_0}{f_0}=\int_0^t g(t-t')\,\frac{{\rm d}\ln X}{{\rm d}t'}\,{\rm d}t' .
\label{eq:superposition}
\end{equation}
A quasi-steady model instead gives the steady value at the instantaneous conditions,
\begin{equation}
\frac{f_{\rm qs}(t)-f_0}{f_0}=\int_0^t g_\infty\,\frac{{\rm d}\ln X}{{\rm d}t'}\,{\rm d}t' .
\label{eq:quasi-steady}
\end{equation}
We subtract equation~(\ref{eq:quasi-steady}) from (\ref{eq:superposition}), substitute \(s=t-t'\), and assume that \({\rm d}\ln X/{\rm d}t\) is nearly constant over the duration of the response. Once \(t\) exceeds \(t_{\rm s}\), the error of the quasi-steady approximation is then
\begin{equation}
\frac{f-f_{\rm qs}}{f_0}=-D_X\,\frac{{\rm d}\ln X}{{\rm d}t},
\qquad D_X=\int_0^\infty\left(g_\infty-g\right){\rm d}s .
\label{eq:qs-error}
\end{equation}
The coefficient \(D_X\) is the area between the step response and its final value. It measures how far \(f\) lags behind its quasi-steady value, and it includes the fast transients as well as the slow part of the response. We write \(D_J\) for \(X=J_{\rm app}\) and \(D_T\) for \(X=T_{\rm bulk}\). When both change at once, the two errors add, since the response is linear. The error stays within 1\,\% when \(X\) changes over a time \(t_X=X/|{\rm d}X/{\rm d}t|\ge t_{1\%}=100\,|D_X|\). Equation~(\ref{eq:qs-error}) also requires \(X\) to change little over the duration of the response, which is a few microseconds at the cathode and a few tens of microseconds at the anode (table~\ref{tab:qs}).

In every case below, the bulk densities follow their equilibrium values \(n_{\rm eq}(T_{\rm bulk})\) after the step, and the case is integrated to the new steady state with \(R\) and \(T_{\rm wall}\) held at their steady values.



\emph{Cathode.} The cathode case of table~\ref{tab:base} was perturbed by a step of \(+2\,\%\) in \(J_{\rm app}\) at fixed \(T_{\rm bulk}\) and, separately, by a step of \(+2\,\%\) in \(T_{\rm bulk}\) at fixed current. After the current step, the ion current density rises by \rev{4.7\,\%}, with \rev{\(D_J=0.45\ \mu\)s}, so that \rev{\(t_{1\%}=45\ \mu\)s}. The heat flux first falls, by up to \rev{31\,\%} within 10~ps, and then rises to \rev{14\,\%} above its initial value. Because this rise is slow and is \rev{seven} times the relative change of the current, \(q_w\) has a larger coefficient, \rev{\(D_J=3.4\ \mu\)s}, and \rev{\(t_{1\%}=0.34\)~ms} (table~\ref{tab:qs}). At fixed current, the cathode layer is almost insensitive to the bulk temperature. A step of \(2\,\%\) in \(T_{\rm bulk}\) changes \(j_i\) by only \rev{0.03\,\%} and \(q_w\) by \rev{0.2\,\%}. Accordingly, \(D_T\) is small, \rev{6~ns} for \(j_i\) and \rev{45~ns} for \(q_w\), and \(t_{1\%}\) is \rev{0.6 and \(4.5\ \mu\)s}, respectively.

\emph{Anode.} The same steps were applied to the anode case of table~\ref{tab:base}, whose response parameters are listed in table~\ref{tab:qs}. After the current step, the heat flux, which is dominated by the electron terms, follows the current almost instantly and rises by \rev{1.1\,\%}, and only a small part of its change is left to the slow part of the response. The ion current density falls by only 0.5\,\%, mostly slowly. Both coefficients \(D_J\) are therefore small, \(-0.19\ \mu\)s for \(q_w\) and \rev{\(-0.92\ \mu\)s} for \(j_i\). In comparison to the cathode, the anode layer depends strongly on the bulk temperature (Section~\ref{sec:res-anode-sweep}). A step of \(2\,\%\) in \(T_{\rm bulk}\) raises \(j_i\) by 14\,\% and \(q_w\) by 3\,\%, and the response is much slower than at the cathode. The coefficients \(D_T\) are therefore nearly two orders of magnitude larger than \(D_J\), so that at the anode it is the bulk temperature, rather than the current, that limits the quasi-steady approximation. The same steps were applied to every case of the anode sweeps of Section~\ref{sec:res-anode-sweep}, and figure~\ref{fig:timescales} shows the resulting \(t_{1\%}\).


Figure~\ref{fig:timescales} summarizes the sweeps. In every case, \(t_{1\%}\) is more than an order of magnitude longer for the temperature step (dashed lines) than for the current step (solid lines), so that the bulk temperature sets the limit for the quasi-steady approximation over the whole range. For the current step, \(t_{1\%}\) has a minimum near \(6\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{A\,m^{-2}}\), where \(D_J\) changes sign, and becomes much shorter at low bulk temperatures, where the slow part of the response almost vanishes. For the temperature step, \(t_{1\%}\) of the ion current density decreases as the bulk temperature rises and changes little with the current, while that of the heat flux decreases with the current and changes little with the bulk temperature.

Both layers can therefore be treated as quasi-steady, to within 1\,\% with respect to electrode ion current \(j_i\) and heat flux \(q_w\), when the current changes over more than about \rev{0.35~ms}. The bulk temperature is irrelevant at the cathode, but at the anode it must change over more than about 2~ms for the heat flux and 11~ms for the ion current to remain quasi-steady. In a typical AC arc at 50 or 60~Hz, the half-period is about 10~ms. If the bulk temperature stays nearly constant over the cycle, both layers can be considered quasi-steady except within about \rev{0.35~ms} of zero current. If the bulk temperature follows the current, the cathode layer is unaffected where as anode layer can no longer be treated as quasi-steady.
